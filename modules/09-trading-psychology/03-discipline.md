# 09.3 — Discipline as a System

## The wrong model

The usual advice is "be more disciplined". It is useless, because it treats discipline as a
quantity you either have or lack, and it puts the burden of control on your worst moment.

Willpower is depletable, state-dependent and unreliable. It is weakest after a loss, late
at night, when tired, and when a position is moving against you — which is to say, exactly
when you need it.

**A plan that requires willpower at the worst moment is a plan that fails at the worst
moment.**

## The right model

Design the environment so that the bad decision is **difficult to execute** and the good
one is the path of least resistance.

The principle is borrowed from every other domain where humans reliably fail under
pressure: you do not train pilots to remember the checklist, you give them a checklist. You
do not ask surgeons to be careful, you count the instruments.

| Weak (willpower) | Strong (structure) |
|---|---|
| "I won't overtrade" | Platform closed outside defined review times |
| "I'll stick to my size" | A printed sizing table; size calculated before the platform opens |
| "I won't move my stop" | Stop placed as a bracket at entry; never touched |
| "I'll stop after losses" | Broker-level daily loss limit, or logging out after two losses |
| "I'll journal every trade" | Journal open on a second screen; trade not counted until logged |
| "I'll check the calendar" | Calendar check is item one on a physical checklist |
| "I'll review monthly" | Recurring calendar appointment with a specific agenda |

## Practical structures

### 1. Checklists

A physical or digital checklist completed before every trade. Not read. Completed.

The [pre-trade checklist](../../templates/pre-trade-checklist.md) takes ninety seconds. Its
value is not the information — you know it all — but the enforced pause and the shift from
automatic to deliberate processing. It is the single highest-leverage habit in this course.

### 2. Precommitment

Decide in advance and remove your later ability to change it.

- Bracket orders at entry, so exits exist before emotion does
- Broker-level deposit limits and loss limits with cooling-off periods
- Position sizes calculated from a table, before the session
- A written plan with amendment rules that forbid changes near a loss

### 3. Friction

Make bad actions harder.

- Do not keep the platform open all day
- Remove the trading app from your phone
- Require the checklist to be completed on paper before any trade
- Impose a mandatory waiting period between identifying a setup and entering

Friction works. The number of impulsive trades is roughly proportional to how easy it is to
place one.

### 4. Routine

A fixed sequence, performed the same way every time. Routine reduces the number of
decisions, and decisions are where errors enter.

**Example daily routine:**

```
1. Check the economic calendar
2. Review open positions against the plan
3. Cancel stale pending orders
4. Record account state: equity, used margin, margin level, total open risk
5. Check for setups on the trading timeframe
6. If a setup exists: complete the checklist, size from the table, place as a bracket
7. Journal any trade placed
8. Close the platform
```

Same order, every day. If step 5 finds nothing, the session takes four minutes and you
close the platform. That is a successful session.

### 5. Measurement

You cannot improve what you do not measure. The process-compliance percentage from Module
08.3 is the key metric, because it is the one thing entirely within your control.

Track it weekly. Below 90%, work on execution rather than on strategy.

### 6. Constraints you cannot override

The strongest controls are ones you cannot reverse in the moment:

- Broker-level loss limits with a cooling-off period
- A separate account with only your allocated risk capital in it, so oversizing is
  physically impossible
- Withdrawing profits regularly so the account stays at its intended size
- Not linking additional funding sources to the trading account

## The right amount of trading

Most retail traders trade far too much. Every trade costs money, and marginal trades have
negative expectancy by definition — they are the ones your rules would have filtered.

**Signs of overtrading:**
- Trading every day regardless of conditions
- Dropping to lower timeframes when no setups appear on yours
- Adding instruments when your main one is quiet
- Trading because you are at the screen rather than because a setup exists
- More than a handful of trades per week on an H4 or D1 strategy

**A strategy that produces four signals a month is not deficient.** Four good trades beat
forty marginal ones, and the arithmetic is not close once costs are included.

The hardest skill in trading is doing nothing. It is also the cheapest one to practise.

## Building it gradually

Do not attempt everything at once. In order:

**Week 1–2:** Complete the checklist before every trade. Nothing else changes.
**Week 3–4:** Journal every trade fully, including the compliance flag.
**Week 5–6:** Fixed routine, platform closed outside it.
**Week 7–8:** Enforce the daily loss limit strictly.
**Week 9+:** Weekly review with compliance measurement.

Each step is small and each becomes automatic before the next is added. This is how the
habits actually form, as opposed to how they are usually attempted, which is all at once
and abandoned within a fortnight.

## When to stop trading

Defined in advance, checked regularly. Stop when:

- You hit your maximum drawdown limit
- Plan compliance falls below 80% and stays there
- You find yourself trading to change how you feel
- You are trading money you cannot afford to lose
- It is affecting your sleep, work, relationships or health
- You are hiding your trading from people close to you

**Stopping is not failure.** Most people who try this stop, and the ones who stop early
keep more of their money. The failure mode is continuing while the evidence accumulates
that you should not.

If any of the last three apply, see the support resources in
[RISK-DISCLOSURE.md](../../RISK-DISCLOSURE.md).

## Key points

- Willpower is weakest exactly when it is needed; do not build a plan that depends on it
- Design structures that make bad decisions hard to execute
- Checklists, precommitment, friction, routine and measurement are the practical tools
- The strongest controls are ones you cannot override in the moment
- Most traders trade far too much; doing nothing is the hardest and cheapest skill
- Build habits one at a time over weeks, not all at once
- Define your stop-trading conditions in advance, and honour them

## Exercise 09.3

**(a)** List every discipline problem you have observed in your own trading. For each,
write a structural countermeasure — something that changes the environment, not something
you intend to do better.

**(b)** Write your daily routine as a numbered sequence. Print it. Use it for two weeks and
record compliance.

**(c)** Identify three sources of friction you can add this week. Implement them.

**(d)** Check whether your broker offers self-imposed loss limits or cooling-off periods.
If so, set them. If not, note what you will use instead.

**(e)** Count your trades over the last month. Compare to what your plan's rules would have
generated if followed exactly. What is the difference, and what does it consist of?

**(f)** Write your stop-trading conditions. Share them with one other person. Set a date to
review whether any have been met.
