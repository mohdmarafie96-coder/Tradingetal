# 09.2 — Emotional States and Tilt

## Tilt

Borrowed from poker. **Tilt is a state in which emotion has replaced process**, and the
defining feature is that decisions are being made to change how you feel rather than to
maximise expected value.

It is not a character failure. It is a predictable physiological response to loss,
uncertainty and threat, and it happens to everyone. What varies is whether there is a
system in place to catch it.

## The spiral

The sequence is remarkably consistent across traders:

1. **A loss.** Within the plan. Unpleasant but normal.
2. **A second loss.** Discomfort rises. The strategy starts to feel wrong.
3. **The urge to recover.** Attention shifts from "what does my plan say?" to "how do I get
   that money back?"
4. **A rule bends.** A marginal setup taken. Size slightly increased. A stop moved.
5. **Another loss, now larger.** Because the position was bigger or the setup was worse.
6. **Escalation.** Size doubled to recover faster. This is the point of no return.
7. **A large loss.** Frequently exceeding every previous loss combined.

**The whole spiral typically takes under two hours.** Most accounts that are destroyed are
destroyed in a single session, not through gradual attrition.

The critical observation: **steps 1 and 2 are normal and unavoidable. Step 3 is where
intervention is possible.** By step 5, you are unlikely to stop voluntarily.

## Recognising it

Tilt is much easier to recognise in advance than in the moment, which is why you write the
list now.

**Physical:** raised heart rate, tension in the jaw or shoulders, leaning toward the
screen, holding your breath, feeling hot.

**Behavioural:** checking positions far more often than usual, opening the platform without
intending to trade, looking for setups on lower timeframes than your plan specifies,
calculating what size you would need to recover.

**Cognitive:** "I need to make this back." "This one is different." "Just one more."
"The market is wrong." "I'll go a bit bigger on this one." "I'll take the stop off just
for now."

**Any of these means stop.** Not "be careful". Stop.

## Emotional states and their effects

| State | Trigger | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Fear** | Recent losses, oversized position | Hesitation, missed entries, exiting winners early, refusing valid setups |
| **Greed** | Winning streak, large unrealised profit | Oversizing, holding past targets, adding to winners without a rule |
| **Hope** | Losing position | Holding past the stop, widening stops, "it will come back" |
| **Regret** | Missed move, closed too early | Chasing, entering late, revenge trading |
| **Boredom** | No setups | Trading marginal setups, dropping to lower timeframes, inventing signals |
| **Euphoria** | Large win | Sizing up, abandoning rules, believing you have solved it |

**Boredom deserves particular attention** because it is the least dramatic and one of the
most costly. A strategy that produces four signals a month leaves a lot of empty time, and
empty time in front of a trading platform reliably generates trades that are not in the
plan. A large proportion of rule violations happen not in distress but in tedium.

The countermeasure is not motivational: it is to not be in front of the platform. If your
plan evaluates at the H4 close, look at it then and close it the rest of the time.

## Fear versus prudence

Not every reluctance to trade is fear. Distinguishing them matters.

**Fear** is diffuse, physical, and unconnected to your rules. You cannot state what is
wrong with the setup; you just do not want to take it.

**Prudence** is specific and articulable. "The setup is present but ATR is double its
normal level and my stop would be wider than my plan allows."

**Test:** can you name the specific rule or condition causing hesitation? If yes, it is
analysis. If no, it is fear, and the usual underlying cause is that your position size is
too large.

**If you are afraid to take a valid setup, reduce your size until you are not.** Fear is
usually a sizing signal.

## The recovery urge

The most dangerous single thought in trading is **"I need to make that back."**

It is dangerous because it is a coherent-sounding goal that has nothing to do with the
market. The market has no memory of your loss and offers no compensating opportunity. The
only thing that changes when you decide to make money back is your willingness to take
worse trades.

**Reframe:** each trade is independent. The previous loss is a sunk cost. The only question
is whether the setup in front of you meets your criteria.

That reframing is easy to state and hard to feel. Which is precisely why the structural
answer — a hard daily loss limit — matters more than the reframing.

## Interrupting the spiral

**The daily loss limit.** Two or three losses, or a fixed percentage, and you stop for the
day. This is the single most effective control available, because it acts at step 3 to 4
and is not a judgement call.

**Physical separation.** Close the platform. Log out. Leave the room. The urge to trade
decays quickly when the means to trade is not immediately present, and it does not decay at
all while you are staring at a chart.

**A mandatory pause.** After any loss, a fixed interval — 30 minutes, or until the next
candle close — before placing another trade. Most revenge trades happen within minutes.

**A pre-trade checklist after a loss.** Physically complete it. The act of working through
a written list re-engages deliberate reasoning and interrupts the automatic response.

**A written commitment.** A card next to your screen: "After 2 losses I stop for the day.
No exceptions." Signed and dated. Sounds trivial. Works better than it should.

## After a large loss

- **Stop trading for at least 24 hours.** Not as punishment. Because your judgement is
  measurably impaired.
- **Do not analyse immediately.** Analysis conducted while distressed produces distorted
  conclusions, usually that the strategy is broken.
- **Write down what happened** factually. What the plan said, what you did, where they
  diverged.
- **Return at reduced size.** Half your normal risk for the next ten trades.
- **Do not change the plan.** Not within 48 hours. Almost every destructive plan change is
  made in this window.

## The loneliness problem

Trading is solitary, and there is no external structure imposing standards. Nobody notices
if you break your rules. Nobody reviews your work. Nobody tells you your sizing is
reckless.

That absence of accountability is a genuine structural weakness, and the countermeasures
are ordinary:

- A trading partner who reviews your journal monthly
- A community where you post trades in advance, publicly, before the outcome
- A written plan you formally review on a schedule
- Telling one person your loss limits

Any external accountability outperforms none. The specific mechanism matters less than the
existence of someone who will see the record.

## Key points

- Tilt is a state where decisions serve emotion rather than expectancy
- The spiral has a consistent sequence and usually completes within a single session
- Intervention is possible at the recovery urge, not later
- Boredom causes as many rule violations as distress
- Fear of a valid setup usually means the position size is too large
- The daily loss limit is the most effective structural control available
- After a large loss: stop for 24 hours, do not analyse, do not change the plan

## Exercise 09.2

**(a)** Write your personal tilt warning signs: physical, behavioural and cognitive. Be
specific to you. Keep the list where you trade.

**(b)** Recall your worst trading session, on demo or live. Reconstruct the sequence
against the seven-step spiral. At which step could you have stopped? What would have had to
be in place?

**(c)** Write your tilt interruption protocol: the specific trigger conditions and the
specific physical actions. Not intentions. Actions.

**(d)** For one week, rate your emotional state 1 to 5 before each trading session and
record it in your journal. At the end, check whether state correlates with plan compliance.

**(e)** Identify one form of external accountability you will actually use, and set it up
this week.

**(f)** Write the sentence you will read when you next feel the urge to make back a loss.
Put it somewhere you cannot avoid seeing it.
