# 11.3 — The Decision

You have written a plan, backtested it, forward tested it for eight weeks, measured the
results and passed the assessment. Now decide.

There are four honest answers, and three of them are not "go live".

## Option 1 — Go live at minimum size

**Only if every one of these is true:**

- [ ] Backtest: 100+ trades, positive expectancy **after** costs
- [ ] Out-of-sample: the two halves of the backtest broadly agree
- [ ] Robustness: performance degrades smoothly under ±20% parameter changes
- [ ] Forward test: 40+ trades, positive expectancy after costs
- [ ] Forward test broadly consistent with backtest
- [ ] **Plan compliance above 95%**
- [ ] Maximum drawdown within your stated limit
- [ ] Plan unchanged for the last 30 trades
- [ ] Capital whose total loss changes nothing material in your life
- [ ] Risk limits with physical enforcement in place
- [ ] Records and tax position understood
- [ ] Broker verified on a regulator's register

**If you go live:**

1. **Minimum size.** The smallest position your risk rule permits. 0.5% risk, not 1%.
2. **Expect it to be worse than demo.** It always is. Real money changes execution.
3. **50 trades minimum before any increase.**
4. **Increase gradually.** 0.5% to 0.75%, not 0.5% to 2%.
5. **Same journal, same reviews, same limits.**
6. **Your drawdown limit ends it.** If you hit it, you stop and return to demo. That is
   what you decided while calm; honour it.

## Option 2 — Keep testing

**The right answer if:**

- Fewer than 40 forward-test trades
- Results ambiguous — expectancy near zero, or dominated by one or two trades
- Compliance between 80% and 95%
- You want more data across different market conditions

Extend the forward test by another eight weeks. No rule changes. More data is the only
thing that resolves ambiguity, and there is no cost to waiting other than impatience.

## Option 3 — Revise and retest

**The right answer if:**

- Expectancy is negative but you have identified a specific, reasoned problem
- The compliance data points to a fixable structural issue
- A cost assumption in the backtest was wrong

**Rules for revision:**

- Change **one thing**. Changing three means you cannot attribute the result.
- Write down the reason, dated, in a new plan version.
- Backtest the revised version from scratch.
- Forward test it for eight weeks from scratch.
- Do not revise more than twice on the same underlying idea. Three revisions of a
  hypothesis that keeps failing is a sign the hypothesis is wrong, not the parameters.

## Option 4 — Stop

**The right answer if:**

- Expectancy is negative and you have no specific, evidence-based fix
- Compliance stayed below 80% despite structural countermeasures
- You cannot give it the time it requires
- The available capital is not genuinely disposable
- It is affecting your sleep, work, relationships or health
- You did not enjoy any of it

**This is a successful outcome.** You spent three months and no money finding out that a
product which loses money for 70–85% of its retail users would also have lost money for
you. The alternative was finding that out at full price.

Most people do not take this option when they should. They fund an account and discover it
over six to eighteen months, and the tuition is substantially higher.

There is no version of this in which stopping is embarrassing. The 70–85% figure exists
precisely because most people do not stop.

## Choosing honestly

Three questions that make the honest answer harder to avoid.

**1. If a friend showed you your own data, what would you tell them to do?**

You will give better advice to someone else than to yourself, because you have no sunk
cost in their situation. Whatever you would tell them is the answer.

**2. What would have to be true for you to stop?**

If you cannot answer specifically, you will not stop, and you should not start.

**3. Are you deciding on the data, or on how you feel about the data?**

Positive expectancy that rests entirely on one outsized winner is not positive expectancy.
Compliance of 88% is not 95%. A drawdown that exceeded your limit exceeded your limit. Read
what the numbers say rather than what you want them to say.

## If you continue: the long view

**Realistic expectations.** A good retail trader might achieve +0.1R to +0.3R expectancy,
producing 10% to 30% annually at 1% risk, with 10% to 20% drawdowns. That is an excellent
outcome and it is far below what the marketing implies.

**Time horizon.** Competence takes years, not months. Your first live year is tuition.

**Position it correctly in your life.** Trading is not a replacement for income, a
retirement plan, or a substitute for long-term diversified investing. It is a serious hobby
with a small chance of modest returns and a real chance of loss. Size it accordingly.

**Keep improving the process.** Journal, review, measure. The traders who last are the ones
who treat it as a practice to be refined rather than a code to be cracked.

**Revisit the exit conditions.** Every quarter, reread what you wrote about when you would
stop. Check whether any of it has quietly happened.

## Your written decision

Write it down. Date it. Include:

1. The decision
2. The evidence, with numbers
3. What would change it
4. The date you will review it

Keep it with your trading plan. When you are tempted to deviate in six months, this
document is the version of you that was thinking clearly.
