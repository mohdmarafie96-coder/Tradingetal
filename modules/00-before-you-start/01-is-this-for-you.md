# 00.1 — Is This For You?

## The arithmetic of the industry

Start from the disclosure every regulated CFD broker in the UK, EU and Australia must
publish: between roughly 70% and 85% of their retail clients lose money.

It is worth sitting with why that number is so stable. It is not that markets were
unusually hard in a particular year. The figure barely moves between bull markets and
bear markets, because the losses are not mostly caused by the market direction. They are
caused by the structure of the product.

Three structural forces work against a retail CFD account:

**1. Costs are continuous and certain; profits are neither.**

Every position pays the spread on entry. Many pay a commission on entry and exit. Any
position held overnight pays financing. These are charged whether the trade wins or
loses. A strategy that is exactly break-even on raw price movement is a losing strategy
after costs, and most strategies are worse than break-even on raw price movement.

**2. Leverage compresses the time available to be wrong.**

Without leverage, a 20% adverse move costs you 20%. At 30:1 leverage, a 20% adverse move
costs you six times your margin, and you were closed out long before it got there. The
same analysis, the same opinion about direction, and the same eventual outcome produce
completely different results depending on how much leverage sat underneath them.
Leverage does not change whether you are right. It changes whether you survive being
temporarily wrong.

**3. The behaviour the product encourages is the behaviour that loses money.**

Instant execution, 24-hour markets, small minimum sizes and a leverage slider all reduce
friction on the decision to trade. Low friction produces more trades. More trades
produce more cost. And frequent small feedback produces the emotional pattern — chase
the loss, cut the winner early — that Module 9 covers in detail.

None of this is a secret and none of it is illegal. It is simply what the product is.

## What separates the minority

Traders who do not lose money over long periods tend to share a small number of
characteristics, and they are mostly unexciting:

- They risk a **small, fixed fraction** of the account per trade, typically 0.5% to 1%.
- They **know their costs** to the currency unit before entering.
- They trade **fewer positions than they want to**.
- They have written rules and follow them when the rules are inconvenient.
- They keep records and review them honestly.
- They treat a drawdown as an expected event with a planned response, not an emergency.
- They have another source of income, so no individual trade needs to work.

Notice what is missing from that list: a better indicator, a faster platform, a secret
pattern, a signal group, a mentor. Those are what the industry sells. They are not what
distinguishes the people who survive.

## Honest reasons to continue

- You want to understand a product you have been marketed, well enough to evaluate it.
- You want to learn how leverage, margin and derivatives actually work, with a real
  worked example rather than an abstraction.
- You want to trade a small, defined amount of risk capital as a serious hobby, fully
  expecting it to cost you money in the first year.
- You work in or near finance and need working literacy in these instruments.

## Honest reasons to stop now

- You need the money. Trading capital under pressure to perform performs worse.
- You are trying to replace a salary within a defined timeframe.
- You are borrowing, using credit, or trading money that is committed to something else.
- You have been shown someone's screenshots and want what they have. Screenshots of
  winning trades cost nothing to produce and select themselves.
- You do not want to do arithmetic. Modules 3, 4 and 6 are arithmetic, and they are the
  parts that matter.

**Stopping is a valid outcome of this module.** If you read the disclosure, read this
lesson, and decide the product is not for you, the course has done its job and saved you
money. That is a better result than most people get.

## A calibration exercise

Before continuing, write down answers to these. Keep them; you will revisit them in
Module 11.

1. How much money could I lose, in total, without it changing anything material about my
   life? Write a number.
2. What do I expect my account to look like after 12 months? Write a number.
3. What would have to happen for me to stop trading? Write a specific, checkable
   condition, not "if it isn't working".
4. Who else is affected if this money disappears?
5. How many hours per week can I genuinely give this?

Most people cannot answer question 3 in a checkable way. That is the one to work on.
