# 10.4 — Scams and Red Flags

The retail trading industry attracts fraud because it combines financial motivation,
technical complexity, and a population predisposed to believe that outsized returns are
achievable. Knowing the standard patterns is directly protective.

## The universal signals

Almost every scam in this space displays at least one of these:

1. **Guaranteed or unusually consistent returns.** No legitimate trading operation
   guarantees returns. "10% per month" is not an ambitious target; it is a claim that,
   compounded, would make someone the wealthiest person alive within a decade.
2. **Pressure and urgency.** Limited-time offers, closing soon, act now. Legitimate
   financial services do not require you to decide quickly.
3. **Lifestyle marketing.** Cars, watches, private jets, laptops on beaches. Advertising
   the results of selling a course, not of trading.
4. **Opacity about method.** "Proprietary algorithm" with no explanation.
5. **Difficulty withdrawing.** The defining symptom. Deposits are instant; withdrawals
   require ever more documentation, or trigger "compliance reviews".
6. **Unsolicited contact.** Cold calls, social media approaches, messages from strangers.
7. **Requests to pay in cryptocurrency.** Irreversible and difficult to trace.

## The common categories

### Fake or clone brokers

A website resembling a legitimate firm, sometimes cloning a regulated broker's name and
licence number. You deposit, you see a platform showing profits, and you cannot withdraw.
The prices shown may be entirely fabricated.

**Defence:** verify the licence on the regulator's own register, and check that the
website domain and contact details match the ones the register lists. Regulators publish
warning lists of clone firms; check those too.

### Signal sellers

A subscription for trade signals, usually with impressive published results.

The economics are the tell. If the signals worked, trading them with borrowed capital would
be far more profitable than selling them for a monthly fee. Published results are typically
selectively reported, include only closed winners, or come from an account that does not
exist.

**Defence:** ask for a full, independently verified track record including every trade,
with drawdowns. It will not be provided.

### Managed accounts and "copy trading" fraud

Someone offers to trade your account for a share of profits. Sometimes legitimate and
regulated; frequently not.

The illegitimate version: the manager trades recklessly because the downside is entirely
yours, or simply takes the money. A profit-share arrangement with no downside for the
manager creates an incentive to maximise variance, which is precisely wrong.

**Defence:** managing money for others requires authorisation in every major jurisdiction.
Verify it. Never give account credentials to anyone.

### Recovery scams

The second layer, targeting people who have already lost money. Someone contacts you
claiming they can recover your funds, for a fee. They cannot. They are frequently the same
people who took the money originally, working from the victim list.

**Defence:** legitimate recovery goes through the broker's complaints process, the
regulator, the ombudsman, or the compensation scheme. None of these charges an upfront fee
to a consumer.

### Bonus traps

A deposit bonus with terms requiring an enormous trading volume before any withdrawal is
permitted — often hundreds of times the bonus amount. The volume requirement guarantees
that costs consume the account before it can be met.

**Defence:** bonuses are prohibited for retail clients under UK, EU and Australian rules. A
firm offering one is outside those regimes, which answers the question.

### Ponzi and pooled investment schemes

An "investment fund" paying returns from new deposits rather than from trading. Returns are
suspiciously smooth and consistent. It collapses when inflows stop.

**Defence:** consistent monthly returns with no losing months is the signature. Real trading
returns are volatile. Verify authorisation, verify the auditor, and be sceptical of anything
you cannot independently confirm.

### Prop firm challenges

A newer category, and a mixed one. You pay a fee to attempt a "funded account challenge"
with profit targets and drawdown limits.

Some are legitimate businesses. Many are structured so that the challenge fees are the
actual revenue, with rules calibrated so that most participants fail. Some do not provide
real capital at all and simulate the funded account.

**Defence:** read the rules carefully, looking for the specific combination of a tight
maximum drawdown with an aggressive profit target and a time limit. Check whether the
"funded" account is real or simulated. Check payout complaints.

### Social media and influencer promotion

Traders with large followings promoting brokers, often on undisclosed affiliate commission,
and posting winning screenshots that are trivially fabricated or heavily selected.

**Defence:** assume affiliate compensation unless disclosed. A screenshot is not evidence
of anything. Treat trading content as marketing by default.

## Romance and long-con investment fraud

Worth naming separately because it is the most financially devastating category and it does
not look like trading fraud.

The pattern: a relationship develops over weeks or months, on a dating app or social media.
Eventually the other person mentions successful trading or crypto investing. They offer to
help. A platform is introduced, showing growing profits. Withdrawals work initially, for
small amounts. Larger deposits are encouraged. Then withdrawal becomes impossible.

**Signals:** someone you have not met in person, introducing an investment; a platform you
cannot find on any regulator's register; small successful withdrawals early; pressure to
invest more; reluctance to video call.

This category takes more money from individuals than all the others combined, and the
losses are frequently life-altering. If someone you have met online introduces you to a
trading platform, the probability that it is fraud is very high.

## What legitimate looks like

- Regulated by a recognised authority, verifiable on the register
- Publishes the percentage of retail clients who lose money
- Prominent risk warnings
- Clear, complete contract specifications
- No bonuses, no guaranteed returns, no performance claims
- Straightforward withdrawals
- No unsolicited contact
- No pressure

Note that legitimate brokers are not charities and their interests are not aligned with
yours. The distinction here is between a lawful business with a conflict of interest and a
fraud. Both require care; only one gives you recourse.

## If you have been scammed

1. **Stop sending money immediately**, including to anyone offering recovery.
2. **Document everything**: communications, transaction records, screenshots, URLs, names.
3. **Report to your national regulator** and to the police or national fraud reporting
   service.
4. **Contact your bank or card issuer immediately.** Chargebacks and payment recalls are
   sometimes possible if you act quickly.
5. **Report to the platform** used for the approach, if applicable.
6. **Do not engage with recovery offers.** They are the second phase of the same fraud.

**Reporting routes:**
- UK: Action Fraud, https://www.actionfraud.police.uk, and the FCA
- EU: national regulator and police; ESMA maintains warning lists
- Australia: ASIC and Scamwatch, https://www.scamwatch.gov.au
- US: FBI IC3, https://www.ic3.gov, plus SEC and CFTC

There is no shame in having been targeted. These operations are professional, well
resourced, and specifically designed to be convincing.

## Key points

- Guaranteed returns, urgency, lifestyle marketing and withdrawal difficulty are the
  universal signals
- Signal sellers have an economic tell: working signals would not be sold
- Recovery scams target people who have already lost money; never pay an upfront fee
- Bonuses are prohibited under UK/EU/AU retail rules, so offering one identifies the firm
- Romance-led investment fraud is the most financially devastating category
- Verify every firm on a regulator's own register, including domain and contact details
- Act quickly on discovery: document, report, contact your bank
