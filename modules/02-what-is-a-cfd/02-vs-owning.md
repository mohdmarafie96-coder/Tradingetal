# 02.2 — CFDs Versus Owning the Asset

The clearest way to understand an instrument is to compare it against its alternatives.
This lesson sets CFDs against direct ownership and against the other common leveraged
products.

## CFDs versus shares

| Dimension | Shares | Share CFDs |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Legal title | None |
| Capital for $10,000 exposure | $10,000 | ~$2,000 (20% margin) |
| Maximum loss | Your $10,000 | Can exceed margin posted |
| Holding cost | None | Nightly financing |
| Shorting | Hard: borrow, locate, recall risk | Trivial, symmetrical |
| Dividends | Received, with tax credits | Cash adjustment; long credited net, short debited gross |
| Voting | Yes | No |
| Time horizon it suits | Years | Days to weeks |
| Tax treatment | Varies; often capital gains | Varies; often different treatment |
| Counterparty risk | Custodian | Broker |

**When ownership is better:** long horizons, dividend income, tax-advantaged accounts,
capital preservation, wanting to be left alone during a drawdown.

**When a CFD is better:** short horizons, shorting, hedging an existing portfolio
without selling it, accessing many markets from one margin pool, or deploying a small
amount of capital deliberately.

**The honest summary:** CFDs are a short-horizon tool. Every structural property —
financing, margin, leverage, no ownership — pushes toward short holding periods. Using
one for a long-term view is fighting the instrument.

## CFDs versus spread betting

Available mainly in the UK and Ireland. Mechanically near-identical: leveraged,
cash-settled, no ownership, same platforms, often the same broker.

Differences:

- **Sizing.** Spread bets are sized in currency per point ("£5 per point") rather than in
  units or lots. Same exposure, different arithmetic.
- **Expiry.** Spread bets have a nominal expiry date, though most are rolled.
- **Tax (UK).** Spread betting profits are generally free of capital gains tax and stamp
  duty, because it is legally a bet. CFD profits are generally subject to CGT. The
  corollary is that spread betting losses are generally not offsettable against gains,
  whereas CFD losses often are.

Tax treatment depends on your circumstances and can change. Get advice; do not rely on
a course.

## CFDs versus futures

| | Futures | CFDs |
|---|---|---|
| Traded on | A regulated exchange | With your broker |
| Counterparty | Central clearing house | Your broker |
| Contract size | Fixed, often large | Flexible, very small available |
| Expiry | Fixed dates | None (rolls or perpetual) |
| Price transparency | Public, consolidated | Broker's own quote |
| Cost structure | Commission, transparent | Spread markup, plus financing |
| Minimum capital | Substantially higher | Very low |
| Regulation | Heavy | Varies |

Futures are the institutional version of the same economic exposure, with better price
transparency and central clearing. CFDs exist largely because futures contract sizes and
account minimums put them out of retail reach.

This comparison is worth internalising: a CFD is a retail-accessible, broker-intermediated
approximation of a futures position, and you pay for the accessibility in transparency
and counterparty risk.

## CFDs versus options

Options give the **right but not the obligation** to transact at a set price. Two
properties change the risk profile fundamentally:

- **Bought options have defined maximum loss** — the premium. A CFD does not.
- **Options decay with time** and price with volatility, so you can be right about
  direction and still lose. A CFD's P&L is linear in price.

An option buyer's worst case is known at entry. A CFD holder's worst case depends on how
far price moves and how quickly the position is closed out. That is a genuine argument in
favour of options for a defined-risk speculation, and a genuine argument against them for
anyone who has not studied them, because they are considerably more complex.

## CFDs versus margin lending

Margin lending — borrowing from a broker to buy more shares than your cash allows — is
the closest non-derivative analogue.

Differences: you retain ownership and dividends with margin lending; margin loan
leverage is typically capped around 2:1 for retail under regulations like Reg T in the
US, far below CFD leverage; and shorting via margin requires locating a borrow. Margin
lending is the more conservative structure, which is why it is the one available to US
retail clients while CFDs are not.

## The comparison that actually matters

Not "CFD versus shares" but **"CFD versus not trading"**.

For most people reading this, the realistic alternatives are:

1. Trade CFDs, with a 70–85% probability of losing money over a year
2. Invest in diversified index funds, unleveraged, over decades
3. Do neither and keep the money

Option 2 has produced positive real returns over essentially every 20-year window in
developed markets, requires almost no time, and carries no risk of ruin. It is boring and
it does not feel like a skill.

This course is not an argument against option 1. It is an argument for choosing it with
accurate information rather than by default. If you trade CFDs, do it with money you have
consciously carved out as risk capital, separate from the long-term investing you should
probably also be doing.

## Key points

- CFDs are structurally short-horizon; financing and margin both penalise duration
- Spread betting is mechanically near-identical with different sizing and tax
- Futures are the transparent, centrally cleared version; CFDs trade transparency for access
- Options offer defined maximum loss; CFDs do not
- The real comparison is against unleveraged long-term investing, and that comparison is
  not favourable on risk-adjusted terms

## Exercise 02.2

**(a)** You hold $30,000 of a share portfolio and expect a short-term market fall but do
not want to sell, for tax reasons. Describe how you would use an index CFD to hedge.
What size? What are the costs and the residual risks? What happens if you are wrong?

**(b)** List three situations where a CFD is genuinely the better instrument than owning
the asset, and three where it is clearly worse. Be specific about why.

**(c)** For your chosen instrument, calculate the total cost of holding one minimum-size
position for one year: spread on entry and exit, commission, and financing at your
broker's current rate. Express it as a percentage of the notional. Then ask yourself what
annual price move you would need just to break even.
