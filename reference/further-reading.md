# Further Reading

Ordered by how useful it is likely to be, with an honest note on each. Primary regulatory
sources come first, because they are the only material in this list with no commercial
interest in your trading.

---

## Primary regulatory sources

These are free, authoritative, and almost never read by retail traders. They are the single
best-value material available.

**ESMA product intervention measures on CFDs** — the European Securities and Markets
Authority's decisions introducing leverage caps, margin close-out and negative balance
protection, with the evidence base that motivated them. Read the analysis of retail outcomes;
it is more informative than any trading book.
https://www.esma.europa.eu

**FCA policy statement PS19/18, "Product intervention measures for retail CFD and CFD-like
options"** — the UK equivalent, with detailed data on retail client outcomes.
https://www.fca.org.uk

**ASIC Report 626, "Review of the Australian CFD market"** — the clearest single document on
what actually happens to retail CFD clients, with distributions of client losses rather than
just averages.
https://asic.gov.au

**Your own regulator's register** — verify any firm before depositing. Links in Module 10.1.

**Your broker's own documents** — the client agreement, order execution policy, contract
specifications, and the published percentage of losing retail accounts. Dry, short, and
directly consequential.

## Books

**Trading in the Zone** — Mark Douglas. On the psychology of accepting uncertainty and
thinking in probabilities. The best-known book on trading psychology and deservedly so.
Repetitive; the core ideas are in the first third.

**Thinking, Fast and Slow** — Daniel Kahneman. Not about trading. The foundational work on
the cognitive biases in Module 09, by the psychologist who did most to establish them. If you
read one book on this list, this is the most broadly useful.

**Fooled by Randomness** — Nassim Nicholas Taleb. On mistaking luck for skill, survivorship
bias, and why track records mislead. Abrasive in tone and correct in substance.

**The Misbehavior of Markets** — Benoit Mandelbrot and Richard Hudson. Why market moves are
far more extreme and more clustered than standard models assume. The mathematical case for
conservative position sizing.

**Market Wizards** — Jack Schwager. Interviews with successful traders. Read with survivorship
bias firmly in mind — the equally diligent traders who failed were not interviewed. Its value
is in the consistency of what they say about risk management, not in any specific method.

**Evidence-Based Technical Analysis** — David Aronson. A rigorous, statistical examination of
whether technical analysis works, by someone sympathetic to it. Difficult, and the best
antidote available to over-fitting.

**Reminiscences of a Stock Operator** — Edwin Lefèvre. A 1923 fictionalised biography of Jesse
Livermore. Enjoyable, frequently quoted, and worth remembering that Livermore was bankrupt
several times and died by suicide. Read it as literature rather than instruction.

**The Psychology of Money** — Morgan Housel. On the behavioural side of financial decisions
generally. Useful for placing trading correctly within a financial life rather than at its
centre.

## On the wider financial picture

Because Module 02.2 argued that the real comparison is against not trading.

**A Random Walk Down Wall Street** — Burton Malkiel. The case for diversified, low-cost,
long-term index investing. Read it alongside this course, not instead of it.

**The Little Book of Common Sense Investing** — John Bogle. Short, direct, and the clearest
statement of the alternative to active trading.

## Data and tools

**Economic calendars** — Forex Factory, Investing.com, and most brokers. Free. Check before
every session.

**TradingView** — charting, with a free tier adequate for this course. Useful bar replay for
manual backtesting.

**Correlation matrices** — published by several charting platforms and broker research pages.
Check more than one time window.

**CFTC Commitments of Traders reports** — weekly futures positioning data.
https://www.cftc.gov/MarketReports/CommitmentsofTraders

## What to be cautious about

**Trading courses and mentorships.** Someone with a reliable edge would trade it rather than
sell access to it. Some educators are honest and competent; the economics still mean that
teaching, not trading, is the business.

**Signal services.** See Module 10.4. The economic argument against them is decisive.

**YouTube and social media trading content.** Enormous variance in quality with no filter.
Screenshots prove nothing, results are self-selected, and much of it is broker affiliate
marketing. Some genuinely good material exists; you cannot reliably identify it as a beginner,
which is the problem.

**Anything promising a specific return.** Covered at length in Module 10.4.

**Backtesting software marketed on its optimisation features.** The easier a tool makes it to
tune parameters, the more efficiently it will help you over-fit.

## How to read all of this

Every source, including this course, has a perspective and may be wrong. The useful habits:

- **Prefer primary sources** — regulators, your broker's own documents, your own data
- **Ask what the author's incentive is** — who pays them, and for what
- **Test claims against your own journal** rather than accepting them
- **Treat a compelling narrative as a warning sign**, not as evidence
- **Be most sceptical of what you most want to be true**

Your own trade journal, honestly kept and regularly analysed, will teach you more about your
trading than every book on this list combined. The books are context. The data is evidence.
